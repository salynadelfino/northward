Your route

Your route to working in the UK

Eight stages, usually over a year or two. Here’s how it generally works — and where to check the parts that change.

Everyone’s route looks a little different

Some nurses get through this in fourteen months. Others take three years, because life happens — a family illness, a pregnancy, an employer who won’t release you, an exam that needs a second attempt. None of that means you have failed at it, and none of it is unusual.

What stays the same is the order. You can’t sit the OSCE before you have a visa, and you can’t get a visa without a role. Knowing the sequence is most of the battle, because it tells you what to work on now and what can safely wait.

If you read one thing

Book your English test. It is the only stage entirely in your hands, it is the one that takes longest to redo, and almost everything else waits on it. Nurses who start there are ahead of people who have been at this a year.

Stage by stage

The eight stages

  1. English test

    IELTS or OET, and there are two separate standards to satisfy — one set by the NMC for registration, one set by the Home Office for the visa. They are assessed differently, and confusing them is the most common and most expensive mistake nurses make. Check both at source before you book anything.

    Official source: NMC — English language requirements · GOV.UK — approved tests

  2. NMC application and CBT

    You create an NMC account, submit your evidence, and sit the computer-based test — part one of the Test of Competence, taken in India. Booking is quick. The revision is where the months actually go, and people underestimate part B.

    Official source: NMC — trained outside the UK

  3. Documents and verification

    Transcripts, your degree and registration certificates, experience and conduct letters, identity documents — often certified, and sometimes verified directly with your council or university. College offices move at their own pace. Start this long before anyone asks you for it.

  4. Test of Competence invitation

    The NMC reviews your application and invites you to the practical part of the Test of Competence. How long this takes varies, and it is largely out of your hands.

  5. A role and a Certificate of Sponsorship

    A UK employer offers you a job and issues your Certificate of Sponsorship. This is the stage the Register exists for: rather than applying into a void, you tell us your profile once and hear from us when a live role suits it.

  6. Visa and travel

    Your visa application, then arrival. The sponsorship side sits with your employer. Requirements are set by the Home Office and change with some regularity, so plan around the current published position.

    Official source: GOV.UK — Health and Care Worker visa

  7. OSCE

    Sat in the UK, at an approved test centre, after you arrive. This is the stage nurses most often underestimate — it is a practical examination and there are a limited number of centres. Most employers support your preparation for it.

    Official source: NMC — Test of Competence

  8. Your PIN, and your first job

    Pass the OSCE and the NMC issues your PIN. You are a registered nurse in the UK. Then comes the part nobody really prepares you for: the first few weeks, the paperwork, and learning how your new workplace actually runs.

Where this stops and the authorities start

Registration rules belong to the NMC. Immigration rules belong to the Home Office. Both change, sometimes at short notice, and neither of them is us.

What we can do is explain how the route generally works, tell you which official source governs each part, and say plainly when the answer is “it depends” or “we don’t know yet”. What we cannot do is advise you on your individual immigration position — that is regulated work in the UK, and it belongs with a registered adviser.
